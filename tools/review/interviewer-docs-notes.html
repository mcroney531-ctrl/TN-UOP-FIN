Behind the Design: WN7 Recovery Strategy

Interviewer note (current draft, with the expanded harness section)

This note explains how I approached the Northstar WN7 case: how I reframed the request, the five decisions that shaped the proposal, how I worked with my coding-agent harness, and what remains uncertain. Northstar Home and everything in the case are fictional.

01From request to performance problem

Dana asked for a five-minute video to help Guides handle upset callers without losing control of the conversation, and she needed something practical for supervisors within days. I treated the video as a proposed solution rather than a diagnosis. Before choosing a format, I worked through the Orbit call sample and operations data, meeting notes and threads, Guide and Lead feedback, and the recovery guidance itself.

The data pointed somewhere specific. In a systematic sample of 204 eligible General Care calls from September 21–30, 115 (56%) went through generic troubleshooting instead of recovery. Of the 159 with a serial check recorded, 70 still ended in generic troubleshooting.

That established a performance gap between identifying an eligible customer and completing the recovery. It didn't establish the cause. A recorded check doesn't show what a Guide saw or understood, and the qualitative sources pointed several ways at once: unclear Plus answers, an Orbit result some Guides couldn't read, uneven access, inconsistent Lead messaging.

"I hear a search problem, an eligibility problem, a conversation problem, and a completion problem."

Priya Bell, Manager of General Care · September 18 shift feedback

Keeping the gap and its possible causes separate shaped everything after it.

02Five decisions that shaped the strategy

These weren't all obvious at the start. Some came early; others changed as the evidence and feedback came in.

  1. Align Leads before expanding to Guides

    Decision
    Make Lead alignment the foundation, with Guide support built on top of it.
    Why
    Guides go to their Leads mid-call, and the release had already relied on Leads to pass the change along without equipping them: two Leads said their Guides first heard about BH-4419 from a reviewer. If Leads give different answers, Guide training can't produce consistent calls.
    What changed
    The first resource became a 25-minute working meeting built on two cases, with two one-page references Leads keep. The rollout aligns each Lead before their own Guides start, and the Lead's role moved from "pass this on" to diagnosing and coaching against one standard.

  2. Address performance, not just knowledge

    Decision
    Treat the gap as a performance problem with learning and non-learning causes.
    Why
    A Lead put it plainly: "People can tell you all five CLEAR steps in a quiz. The issue is the pivot from listening to acting." Several barriers weren't about knowledge at all: no approved answer for a pending Plus credit, an Orbit result some Guides couldn't locate, possible permission gaps. A video can communicate the process; it can't fix those.
    What changed
    The package pairs learning with in-the-moment support (the Guide Call Path) and makes operational validation part of the plan, naming who must confirm Plus guidance, Orbit access and delivery before any Guide sees it. The video wasn't discarded: the plan keeps a narrower Orbit tool walkthrough only as an optional item, not a launch prerequisite, and only if an approved screen reference and General Care access are confirmed.

  3. Differentiate support by audience

    Decision
    Design around what each audience has to do, not one course for everyone.
    Why
    Leads need to recognize good execution, diagnose a breakdown before coaching it, and tell a performance gap from an operational blocker. Guides need the next step at the moment of decision and a chance to practice the hard call before it counts.
    What changed
    Leads get the meeting, a Quick Reference and Coaching Cues. Guides get the Call Path and a short individual practice, connected through the huddles Leads already run with one Lead-facilitated slide. The practice, Make the Call, centers on a Not eligible result: the symptoms fit the WN7 pattern but the serial doesn't qualify, so the Guide has to make and explain the decision without promising an unsupported recovery. A Guide asked for exactly that: "A practice case with an ineligible unit would help, because the easy example always has a happy ending."

    How it evolved: I first proposed a short Guide practice huddle. It became an individual digital scenario, which kept practice out of an extra live session while the team huddle and slide stayed as the place Leads introduce the change.

  4. Minimize operational disruption

    Decision
    Treat time off the phones as a design constraint, not an afterthought.
    Why
    There are roughly 900 hourly Guides on rotating schedules, Specialist Care can absorb only a limited number of WN7 transfers, and Workforce warned that a broad session would push abandonment higher.
    What changed
    Every component is small and fits existing routines: a 25-minute Lead meeting, reinforcement in existing huddles, a 5–7 minute individual practice in scheduled work time (subject to Workforce scheduling), and a one-page Call Path for live calls. The aim was less disruption without thinner support.

    How it evolved: GPT first proposed a 25-minute Lead meeting. I cut it to 20 because Leads have little spare time, then restored 25 to give the case discussion and alignment room, accepting the cost in Lead time on the grounds that one session replaces fragmented coaching later. The agenda settled at 3 · 9 · 10 · 3, with the middle 19 minutes run as one exercise.

  5. Pilot, evaluate, then scale

    Decision
    Start with two or three representative Lead teams, subject to Workforce approval, and expand by wave only on evidence.
    Why
    The intervention depends on operational conditions that aren't confirmed yet, and completion data alone wouldn't show whether calls changed. Marisol said she needed "a reason to believe it will work."
    What changed
    Five phases, each closed by a proceed, adjust or pause decision. Expansion needs Care leadership's judgment and Workforce's approval for each wave, and delivery is reported as reach, never as proficiency. Fewer avoidable escalations to Specialists is a hoped-for benefit, not a demonstrated outcome.

Learner-facing samples to look at first: the Guide Call Path (performance support at the point of need), then Make the Call (how that extends into practice). The Huddle Sequence Slide shows the Lead-to-Guide connection.

These five decisions had the greatest influence on the overall strategy, but they represent only part of the design process. Additional decisions involving scenario selection, coaching structure, resource formats, operational constraints, and implementation details are documented in the accompanying Design Decision Log.

03How I worked with the coding-agent harness

I approached this project with AI as a collaborative development environment, not simply a way to generate finished materials. I wanted to retain ownership of the analysis and design decisions while using coding agents to make research, experimentation, implementation, and revision more efficient.

Building a persistent project memory

Before receiving the full assignment instructions, I had already established a GitHub repository around an idea: maintaining a living record of project decisions alongside development, rather than relying on conversational history.

As the project expanded, that documentation became essential infrastructure. Claude Code increasingly maintained the records as part of its regular workflow, while I directed how the documentation system evolved.

I introduced RESOURCES.md to track the growing collection of learning resources and their intended purposes. I also established QUOTES.md as a reusable reference for evidence I had flagged during my investigation. DECISIONS.md preserved choices, rationale, and changes in direction.

The structure wasn't fixed from the beginning. An early ARCHITECTURE.md document was eventually retired because the design had developed through interconnected resource and meeting decisions. The challenge became assembling those decisions into a coherent strategy rather than maintaining a separate architecture document.

This living documentation helped both Claude and me revisit earlier reasoning, check for inconsistencies, and maintain continuity across sessions.

Building small tools to improve the work itself

I also used coding agents to create lightweight, purpose-built interfaces for different stages of the project.

The first was an inbox-style reader for exploring the original 75 source items. I wanted the experience to feel like working through actual organizational communications, not reading a flattened collection of documents. I could follow the chronology, star significant messages and threads, archive others, and annotate observations.

Once I began developing the learning strategy, I had Claude consolidate my flagged evidence into QUOTES.md. I used that reference throughout design to check for blind spots and whether the concerns surfaced during the needs assessment were being addressed.

A separate project organizer helped me track how the strategy and individual resources were developing. I could update it, export its state, and bring it into later sessions as a shared reference.

For editorial review, I used another custom artifact that let me highlight passages, annotate specific concerns, and export a targeted revision list. This became particularly useful when refining the pre-read email and other documents through multiple review passes.

These weren't large software projects. With coding-agent assistance, a useful tool could often be developed in one to three prompts. That made it practical to build small interfaces around the way I wanted to investigate, organize, and review information rather than adapt my process to a generic tool.

Separating collaboration from implementation

I used GPT and Claude Code in complementary roles.

GPT was a strategic collaborator and independent source of critique. I used our discussions to analyze the evidence, challenge assumptions, compare alternatives, refine the learning strategy, and develop implementation briefs. GPT also helped create a coordinated set of image-generation prompts for the resource thumbnails.

Claude Code worked directly with the repositories, developing the resources and custom tools, checking source materials, maintaining documentation, identifying inconsistencies, and implementing revisions.

The responsibilities weren't rigid. Both models contributed suggestions and challenged decisions. I evaluated those contributions, determined what to adopt or change, and approved the direction of the work.

Checking the work instead of assuming the output was correct

The workflow followed a recurring pattern: develop, inspect, challenge, revise, and lock.

Several corrections illustrate why that mattered. An early planning brief used approximately 600 Guides; Claude checked the source packet and identified the correct figure of approximately 900. During slide development, GPT suggested wording that made the Plus credit sound conditional. Claude flagged the conflict with Finance and Product Operations guidance, and the requirement for both replacement and credit was preserved.

Other reviews caught unsupported policy assumptions and attribution errors. Rather than filling those gaps with plausible answers, I kept the uncertainty visible and identified where stakeholder confirmation was required.

Automated checks helped identify implementation issues, but I treated reported test results as evidence of what had been checked—not proof of real-world usability or learning effectiveness. Independent user testing and screen-reader validation remain outstanding.

The value of the harness wasn't that AI eliminated the need for judgment. It made it possible to investigate more thoroughly, experiment more quickly, maintain a traceable record of decisions, and apply more deliberate review throughout the design process. 04Intentional tradeoffs

  • No simultaneous launch. A full-population rollout would be faster to announce, but it would cost phone time and leave no way to learn what works first.
  • Lightweight resources over a course. Each resource has one job and points to Beacon as the single authority, so nothing competes with it as a source of procedure.
  • Dependencies stay visible. Where the case left something unresolved, the materials say who has to confirm it rather than resolving it for them.

05What I'd validate

ReadinessAre Plus guidance, Orbit workflow and access, the Lead support route and fallback, practice delivery, and pilot scheduling confirmed by their owners?
PerformanceOn reviewed calls, are Guides recognizing, verifying, deciding, completing, communicating and documenting as intended?
Operational impactAre complete recoveries increasing without unacceptable effects on handle time, wait and abandonment, or Lead and Specialist load?

A small pilot can surface problems and give directional evidence. It can't, on its own, prove the intervention caused an improvement, especially against a baseline whose eligibility was classified after the fact.

06What remains uncertain

  • The root cause of the gap, and how it splits across knowledge, tool access, policy clarity and workflow.
  • How well Leads themselves know the WN7 path. The packet has no baseline, and what Leads asked for (cues, clips) may not be all they need.
  • How to handle a pending Plus credit, the eligibility-review follow-up, and the fallback when a Lead is unavailable.
  • Whether Sana can host the free-text practice natively, or only link to it.
  • Whether the meeting, slide and practice change what happens on live calls. That's what the pilot is for.

07What I'd do next in a real engagement

None of this has happened yet; it's what the proposal sets up.

  • Confirm policy and operational ownership, starting with Plus handling and Orbit access.
  • Observe real Guide and Lead workflows rather than relying on meeting notes.
  • Validate the Call Path, the practice model answers and the Lead materials with Quality and Specialist Care.
  • Test usability with a few Guides and Leads, including screen-reader use.
  • Agree the evaluation baseline and its limits with Quality.
  • Pilot, gather evidence, and iterate before expanding.

Closing reflection

The request was for a video. What the evidence described was a team being asked to carry out a new process under time pressure, with partial answers and uneven support. I tried to design for that reality: understand what each person has to do on the call or in the review, give them support at that point, and set up a way to see whether performance actually changes. Producing content was the smallest part of the job.

BLead alignment and coaching

  1. B4Lead-anchored, but not a gate on Guides

    Designer decision

    Raised by: Me; Claude (evidence); GPT (reframe) · Decided by: Me

    Decision
    Anchor the design in Leads while Guide support develops in parallel.
    Rationale
    Guides can't carry out a process their Leads haven't been aligned on, and Leads can't give consistent feedback without knowing what to look for. The release had already relied on Leads without equipping them.
    Alternative / tradeoff
    GPT's three-layer model (foundation, reinforcement, execution) was noted but not adopted. "First" was deliberately not treated as a gate.
    Consequence
    Lead alignment leads the rollout, and Guides apply approved guidance on live calls right away.
    Working log: D45

  2. B5Calibrate through cases, not a Lead knowledge test

    Designer decision

    Raised by: Me (the question); GPT (assessment idea); Claude (evidence review) · Decided by: Me (working answer)

    Decision
    Treat Lead readiness as a gap in what reached Leads, and calibrate through realistic cases rather than testing WN7 knowledge.
    Rationale
    The packet showed strong knowledge in some Leads and uncertainty in others, with no baseline. Leads asked for cues and clips, not WN7 training.
    Alternative / tradeoff
    A three-dimension Lead readiness model with a WN7 knowledge assessment (GPT), not adopted because it would solve an unestablished problem. The gap between what Leads asked for and what they may need stays an open uncertainty.
    Consequence
    The meeting opens with a 3-minute verified-current-state refresh, and understanding shows up in the case discussion.
    Working log: D66

  3. B6Meeting length: 25, then 20, then 25 minutes

    Proposed · needs approval

    Raised by: GPT (25 minutes; agenda splits) · Decided by: Me (20, then back to 25; the final split)

    Decision
    A 25-minute working meeting, agenda 3 · 9 · 10 · 3, with the middle 19 minutes run as one exercise.
    Rationale
    Twenty minutes left too little room for the cases and alignment. The extra time goes to discussion, not presentation.
    Alternative / tradeoff
    Twenty minutes, which better fit the evidence on Lead time ("one Lead could spare ten minutes"; Priya's "smallest manager behavior"). The trade-off is Lead time against one session replacing fragmented coaching later.
    Consequence

    Working log: D69, D79

  4. B7Two cases, and no second Plus case

    Designer decision

    Raised by: Me (two cases); GPT (move the ineligible case); Claude (candidates, composite label) · Decided by: Me (locked)

    Decision
    Case A: a documented strong recovery where the Plus credit hadn't applied. Case B: a labeled composite in which the serial was checked but troubleshooting continued, ending with what the Lead would check first.
    Rationale
    One case shows the standard; the other practices diagnosing before coaching, which the 70-of-159 pattern called for.
    Alternative / tradeoff
    A second Plus case, rejected because Plus was already covered and it would have cost the diagnostic practice. The ineligible case moved to Guide practice.
    Consequence
    Case B is presented as a call record built from real data fields and labeled a composite, not as a real call.
